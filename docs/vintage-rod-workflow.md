# Vintage rod workflow

This page explains the workflow for evaluating vintage rods, documenting and testing them, and promoting the documentation to the public site (the repo).

This workflow keeps the rod collection complementary to the reel archive and the focus of the Vintage Reel project vintage-reel-centric by acquiring vintage rods selectively.

## Workflow

1. Evaluate rod for the collection.

    See [vintage rod collecting standards](rod-collecting-standards.md).

2. Acquire rod, then add a dated purchase note to vintage-rod-log.md.

3. When rod is received, inspect and doc:

    - Write a set of rough bench notes.

    - Visually inspect the blank / decals, handle / reel seat, and guides / wraps, note condition.

    - Check functionality of reel seat, bushing, and any ferrules, note condition.

4. Photograph the rod, including all the aspects and parts covered in vintage-rod-log.md.

    Save image files in the media folder for the appropriate rod.

## Colors, measurements, materials

  - Blank color, diameter at grip and just below tip, length of blank, and total measured length.

  - Each guide: style, ring material, wrap color, trim bands, and guide spacing from butt cap.

  - Handle length, cork style, reel-seat material and any markings.

  - Butt-cap material and markings.

## Testing

Start with recommendations in maker documentation if available, if not use Shakespeare's balanced tackle tables as a starting point - these can be found in the Vintage Reel repo in the background pages for each reel type as well as the overview pages for each reel. Treat the upper range of suggested weights as a careful test range.

Start progressively - for example, for a rod tentatively in the "heavy" category which should be capable of handling 3/4 oz. + weights:

  - Cast a 1/2 oz practice plug or sinker with a smooth lob.

  - Move to 3/4 oz and watch the top third of the blank during the cast.

  - If it loads smoothly without the tip folding excessively or the butt feeling overstrained, try 1 oz—but avoid snap-casting.

  - Stop if you hear guide-wrap ticking, see stress whitening/cracks, or feel a sharp “hinge” in the blank.

Test-cast with a range of recommended weights, line type / diameter, reel, cast style, and subjective load point.

Enter results in rod doc in workstpace `rods/`

## Cleaning guides

The procedure depends on the condition of the guides and wraps. If the wraps are in excellent condition, this is a conservation job, not a “make the guides look new” job. For only light rust, use controlled dry abrasion and avoid tape-plus-liquid cleaners entirely.

A pencil can be used with fine steel wool to clean the inner surface of the guides. Use the least aggressive material possible, keep every operation dry, and treat only the exposed metal—not the guide feet or wrap edge.

Best low-risk approach

1. Examine each guide under strong light and magnification:

    - Is it only orange/brown surface haze, or has rust created pits?

    - Is the rust confined to the exposed loop, or is it creeping down at the guide foot beneath the wrap?

    - Do you see any lifting, cracking, or darkened varnish at the wrap edge?

    - Are the guides plain steel, plated steel, or chrome / nickel plated?

    If it is a light bloom on otherwise smooth wire, cleanup is worthwhile. If the corrosion has pitted the wire or starts from beneath the wraps, you can improve appearance but cannot fully stop it without eventually rewrapping or replacing the guide.

    Advice from classic-rod restorers similarly distinguishes superficial rust—often cleanable with #0000 steel wool—from deeper pitting, where removal leaves rough metal and replacement becomes the better long-term solution.

2. Use the gentlest abrasives in this progression:

    - Use #0000 steel wool, pulled through the guide as a thin twisted strand.

    - If necessary, a tiny piece of gray ultrafine Scotch-Brite or a very fine synthetic polishing pad, but only on exposed metal.

    - Stop as soon as the orange rust is gone and the guide feels smooth.

    A small loop or “shoeshine” strand of #0000 steel wool can be drawn back and forth through the guide ring, which is an established technique for light surface rust on old rod guides.

    A pencil has a particular advantage on the inside of small snake guides: it gives you mechanical control and keeps fingers and the abrasive away from the wraps. Avoid pressing hard enough to burnish through any original plating.

Do not use: medium steel wool, sandpaper, wire wheels, Dremel tools, naval jelly, Evapo-Rust, vinegar, lemon juice, Coca-Cola, rust converters, or paste metal polishes near intact vintage wraps. Even if tape protects most of the thread, liquid can wick along the guide foot or through a minute break in the old wrap finish. Abrasive particles and polish residue can also lodge at the wrap edge.
A controlled bench workflow

Specific recommendations for plated guides

For the guides themselves, I would now use this order:

    Dry soft brush first.
    A clean, soft sable-style artist brush, camera-lens brush, or very soft toothbrush can lift surface dirt from the stamped lettering, inside corners, and guide-to-insert junction.

    Wooden pick for compacted deposits.
    Use a sharpened bamboo skewer, orange-wood manicure stick, or toothpick to gently flick crusted material from the metal-to-insert seam. Work under magnification. Do not pry against the insert and do not work downward toward the wraps.

    Dry microfiber or cotton patch.
    Lightly rub exposed bright metal only. Wrap a small piece around a toothpick or a suitably shaped wood dowel for precise access.

    Reserve 0000 steel wool for the inside line path only, if needed.
    If the metal inner ring itself has a localized rust bump or rough spot, use a minuscule, dry piece of 0000 steel wool around a pencil or wood mandrel with almost no pressure. Make only a few passes, then inspect.

    Remove all debris.
    Use a bulb blower, followed by a fine brush and a dry microfiber wipe. Do not use compressed-air cans close to the wraps; propellant or force can drive debris into crevices.

    Do a snag test.
    Pull cotton thread, a cotton ball tuft, or a narrow strip of nylon stocking around the full line-contact path. Any repeated snag is more important than cosmetics: it may indicate a chipped insert, damaged inner metal edge, or corrosion roughness.

Specific recommendations for non-plated guides

Here is the procedure I would use, one guide at a time.

Dry cleaning

    Lay the rod horizontally with the guide you are treating at the top.

    Put a folded piece of clean white paper or a thin Mylar/plastic shield under the guide loop, between the loop and the blank if the geometry allows.

    Use a short, narrow twisted strand of 0000 steel wool to polish the inside of the loop.

    Use a small piece of the same wool, pinched in fine forceps or hemostats, to touch the outside of the loop and frame.

    Work away from the wrap whenever possible: from the center of the guide loop outward, rather than scrubbing toward the guide foot.

    Vacuum or gently blow away loose steel-wool fragments as you go. A soft artist’s brush also works well.

    Wipe the exposed guide metal with a dry microfiber cloth or a tightly folded lint-free cotton patch.

A key practical issue with steel wool is stray fragments: remove them carefully, especially around the guide foot and wrap edge, so none remain to rust later.
Inspect for line damage

After the rust is removed, test every guide—not just the visibly rusty ones—with a length of white cotton thread, cotton ball fiber, nylon stocking, or a strip of microfiber cloth.

Pull it lightly through and around the guide:

    If fibers snag, the guide may have a burr, a pit, or damaged plating.

    If it feels smooth but shows dark staining, clean once more with dry 0000 wool and retest.

    If it catches repeatedly at one point, do not keep polishing indefinitely. That guide is a candidate for eventual replacement if the rod will be fished.

Rod-care guidance commonly recommends a cotton test for rough spots, corrosion, cracks, and edges that can damage line.
If you want a little more protection

Once the guide is clean and dry, I would normally leave it alone rather than put a visible coating on it. Old wraps are your priority, and any oily or waxy material near them can migrate, collect dust, or create a future adhesion problem if a wrap ever needs repair.

If the rod will be displayed or stored in Florida humidity, you could apply an extremely small amount of a corrosion-inhibiting wax to a cotton swab, touch only the exposed wire, then immediately buff it nearly dry with clean cotton. Do this only after a test on the least conspicuous guide, and keep the swab well away from the wrap edge. The result should not look wet, feel oily, or leave a residue on the blank.

For a rod you intend to fish, the simplest prevention is more important:

    Wipe guides dry after use.

    Never return it to a tube or cloth bag while damp.

    Store it in a stable, dry indoor environment rather than a garage, shed, or vehicle.

    Inspect guide feet and wraps periodically, because corrosion under wraps often announces itself first as staining or a tiny finish crack at the foot.

A useful stopping point

For this particular rod, I would call the job successful when:

    Orange surface rust is removed or substantially reduced.

    The guide feels smooth under the cotton test.

    The guide retains its original finish, even if it has small dark age marks.

    No fibers, abrasives, liquids, or polish residues have contacted the wrap.

    The wraps remain exactly as they were when you began.

A little honest darkening or minor spotting on original guides is preferable to a bright, over-polished result that looks out of place against otherwise excellent original wraps. Given that this St. Croix may become a centerpiece of your selective vintage-rod collection, preserving that original balance is probably the better aesthetic and historical choice.
