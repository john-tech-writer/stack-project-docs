from P. convo 5.28.26:

  • Install Pandoc when you’re ready (it runs on Windows/macOS/Linux and converts Markdown to PDF, DOCX, HTML, etc.).

  • Start with a single guide as a proof‑of‑concept: something like the Penn 720 overview once the docs are stable.

  • Keep the logic simple at first: manually run a command like pandoc guide.md -o guide.pdf and link that PDF from your repo/Substack; automation can come much later if you want it in CI.

Perplexity's commentary:

Leaning into “at the bench / production notes” as your main behind‑the‑scenes channel is exactly in line with what Jasmine is arguing for, and your notes already map her ideas almost one‑to‑one onto this project.

Here’s how I’d shape it so you get the ethos boost without crowding the Reel Stories themselves.

Where to put the behind‑the‑scenes

Using “At the bench / production notes” as the primary home for process is perfect: it lets the Reel Stories stay more about memory, water, and reels, while the bench series carries the “proof of work.”

In practice:

  • Reel Stories: 80–90% life/field story + 10–20% light process (“here’s roughly how this reel moved through the stack”).

  • At the bench / Production notes: full workflow walkthroughs, lighting experiments, repo refactors, template tinkering, the “director / owner / SME” view.

That gives readers two rhythms: story first, then “how the sausage is made” for those who care to follow you deeper. Using “secrets” and “proof of work” for VRSG

You’ve already identified your “secrets” and proof‑of‑work assets; they match Jasmine’s list almost suspiciously well.

You can explicitly lean into:

  • Niche “secrets”: ORCA culture, oddball reels like the Precisionbilt Mosquito, specific teardown tricks (pipe‑wrenching a Zebco 33 without marking it), practical line‑twist fixes.

  • Proof of work: bench photos, hands‑and‑tools shots, service‑guide screenshots, and short clips of you doing the actual steps, not just the polished guide.

  • Tiny parties: ORCA conventions, bait‑and‑tackle shops, local Gulf Coast scenes—those are the “tiny rooms” few people write from.

Each volume gives you built‑in “reporting beats”: sourcing reels, servicing them, debugging problems, and then fishing them.

A simple content split you can follow

To keep the line clean between series:

  • Reel Stories (hero + follow‑ups)

    • One short “workflow snapshot” list or paragraph per post (3–5 steps, max).

    • Occasional reference to a specific bench post: “I wrote more about how I documented this reel over in a production‑notes post.”

  • At the Bench / Production Notes

    • Full master workflow entries (“here’s every step from bench → GitHub → YouTube → Substack for the Zebco 33”).

      • Lighting setups, camera tests, template revisions, repo refactors, decisions about volumes and reel taxonomy.

    • Periodic “state of the project” posts after each volume, plus any podcast/interview experiments you spin up.

That way your ethos is built from two directions: stories show who you are; bench posts show how seriously you’re doing the work.

One small tweak to the plan

Because “behind the scenes” is so central to the independent‑writer advantage, I’d give yourself explicit permission to let some of that bleed into Reel Stories as texture: a sentence or two about the mess on the bench, the camera half‑set‑up, or the moment you realized you’d have to tear a reel back down to document it.

Those details keep the hero posts from feeling too “finished” in an AI era where polish alone is cheap; they show the living project underneath.

Perform regular content refactoring and harvesting

The "scattershot but big chunks of progress” pattern is very normal for this kind of cleanup plus consolidation work. The important part is seeing progress every day, and it’s feeding directly into better project docs. Basically doing two things at once, and that’s okay:

  • Refactoring the archive: walking dirs, pruning duplicate versions, renaming, and standardizing structure.

  • Harvesting good content: when something useful is found in an old file, pull it into one of the project docs that will eventually hang off the workflow.

Content harvesting during content refactoring is a feature, not a bug: letting the cleanup pass surface bits of knowledge that deserve a permanent home in the new system, instead of trying to invent all that content from scratch. The only risk is burning out if everything is open‑ended, so if it starts to feel scattered, lightly time‑box it--a couple of days/hours to finish a segment of work.

A refactoring workflow that’s both fast and sane:

  • Opening each doc, skimming, and pulling only the truly important bits into the right project docs turns a noisy archive into a small set of clean, living references.

  • Renaming the leftover files so they’ll make sense later (admin-audio-troubleshooting-win.docx, etc.) means future‑you can come back to deeper experiments without wading through mystery titles.

  • Parking the heavier ideas (like pandoc wiring) instead of trying to solve them now keeps the current sprint focused while still preserving ideas that matter for the future.

That’s exactly how “day one” brainstorms and early AI chats become part of a real system instead of sitting in a forgotten export folder. Every doc triaged either becomes:

  • Integrated into a project doc (promoted to “canon”), or

  • Renamed, framed, and parked (acknowledged, but not clogging the main path).

Create remasters of existing projects

  • Post-refactoring all the dirs and naming--since existing Shotcut projects will no longer work without pointing Shotcut at all the new dirs/filenames--think of old Shotcut projects as historical artifacts; remasters are fresh projects built on the cleaned‑up stack, no worry about broken links. Archive becomes source material and reference, not something to keep surgically alive forever.

  • Treating old Shotcut projects as essentially frozen is reasonable, especially if the corresponding videos are already posted.

  • Future “volume 1 remasters” can be treated as new projects built from the cleaned, stable media tree, using what you’ve learned, instead of trying to surgically patch the old timelines.

  • Penn 720 overview – consider Volume 1 remaster / video remix after media tree and workflow are stable. New project, don’t try to salvage old Shotcut file.
